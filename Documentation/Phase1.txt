# Phase 1: Building the Misconception Taxonomy

## What we're building
A short, curated list (8–15 entries) of the specific, recurring ways students
get a topic wrong. Each entry ("node") is tagged to one of four dimensions:
**completeness, accuracy, clarity, coherence**. This list is the reference the
diagnosis agent checks a student's explanation against — instead of the AI
vaguely deciding "this seems incomplete," it points to a specific, named,
pre-defined gap.

As a byproduct, this same extraction produces reusable examples for the
Socratic agent (Phase 3) — see "Secondary output" below.

## Why we need this before writing any agent code
Without a fixed list of known gaps, the diagnosis step has nothing concrete to
compare against — it would be guessing in an unanchored way. This taxonomy is
the "answer key" that makes the whole system's diagnosis interpretable rather
than a black box.

## Where the raw material comes from — and a correction to our first approach
We use SocraTeach, a public dataset of real teacher-student math dialogues.

Our first attempt extracted candidates from each problem's `steps` field — a
short, fixed checklist of guiding questions per problem (e.g. "find the total,"
"find the amount given away," "find the remainder"). This turned out to be too
thin a source: `steps` is a generic, pre-planned decomposition of the problem,
not a reaction to an actual student error, and for simple arithmetic problems
it often contains no diagnosable reasoning at all — just a sequence of "what
number do you need next" prompts. It also produces very little data.

**Corrected approach:** we extract from `dialogues` instead — specifically,
consecutive `(student turn, teacher's next turn)` pairs. The teacher's message
immediately following a student's response is a real reaction to something
that student said — a correction, a probing question, a request for
clarification. That reaction is a much more direct and specific signal of a
real misconception than a generic checklist, and there is far more of it:
every dialogue variant of every problem contributes multiple such pairs,
giving hundreds of candidates per topic instead of a handful.

## The method, in plain terms
1. Filter the dataset down to problems on our chosen topic (keyword match on
   `question` + `analysis`).
2. For every dialogue, walk through consecutive turns. For each student turn,
   pair it with the teacher's very next message.
3. For each pair, ask: **"why did the teacher respond this way — what was
   wrong, missing, unclear, or disconnected in what the student just said?"**
   Write that down in plain language (`inferred_gap`), and classify it into
   one of the four dimensions (`dimension`).
4. Skip pairs that don't reveal a real reasoning gap — small talk,
   encouragement, or a teacher simply moving the conversation forward with no
   correction. Not every pair is usable, and that's expected; be selective
   rather than forcing a label onto every row.
5. Many pairs turn out to describe the same underlying mistake in different
   words — we merge those into one final entry during consolidation.
6. The result is a short list of distinct, named misconceptions — our
   taxonomy — saved as a simple YAML file.
7. A second person independently labels a sample of the same data, and we
   compare notes — this catches cases where our dimension definitions were too
   fuzzy, and makes the final taxonomy more trustworthy.

## Secondary output: Socratic-agent examples
While reading each `(student_said, teacher_followup)` pair for the taxonomy
label, we also mark a `good_socratic_example` flag (yes/no): does the
teacher's response guide with a targeted question, rather than just stating
the correct answer directly? Pairs marked "yes" become topic-specific few-shot
examples for the Socratic agent later (Phase 3), alongside the separate DPO
dataset's more general Socratic-vs-direct-answer contrast pairs. This costs
almost nothing extra since we're already reading the pair closely for the
taxonomy label — it just saves re-reading the same data again later.

## Worked example
> Student said: "70 minus 10 is 60, so that's the answer for the dogs and
> cats."
> Teacher's next message: "You said dogs and cats — the question only asks
> about dogs. Which animal were we tracking here?"

→ Inferred gap: conflates the two animal categories instead of tracking only
  the one the question asks about.
→ Dimension: **clarity** (the student's statement is ambiguous about which
  quantity is being referred to).
→ good_socratic_example: yes (teacher asks a targeted question rather than
  just stating "it's dogs, not both").

## Output of this phase
- `taxonomy.yaml` — 8 to 15 entries, each with an id, a short description, and
  a dimension tag. This is the fixed reference point for every following
  phase of the project.
- A tagged subset of `(student_said, teacher_followup)` pairs marked as good
  Socratic examples, held for reuse in Phase 3.
